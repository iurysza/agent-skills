# References

Rules in [SKILL.md](SKILL.md) are paraphrases of the public pages below, plus layout observed on the opening of one public PDF. They are not quotations and not an official style guide. If a page disagrees with the skill, the page wins. Say which page you followed.

Lees's interview, the election colour sheet, the FT vocabulary page, the GIJN account of Burn-Murdoch, Cox's Simply Statistics interview, the Times byline and promotion notes, and the staff directory pages were fetched in full. Leo, Atkinson, Selby-Boothroyd, and Pearce were available only as indexed excerpts after the Medium pages timed out. Rules taken from those four are limited to the excerpts. Tse's three rules are the text of his [2016 slides](https://github.com/archietse/malofiej-2016).

People listed here are the requested shortlist. Extra bylines appear only where a public note supplied a rule.

Leo, [Mistakes, we've drawn a few](https://medium.economist.com/mistakes-weve-drawn-a-few-8cdd8a42d368) (Economist on Medium, 27 March 2019). The fetch of the full article timed out. What follows is from the portion the search index returned, which covers the setup and the first two groups:

- She sorts failures into misleading, confusing, and failing to make a point. The third group's examples were not in the retrieved text. Do not invent them.
- A truncated bar scale both hid a large value and exaggerated the smaller ones. The redraw showed the long bar in full.
- A double scale whose two axes stepped by the same number of units made two series look more tightly matched than a proportional reading supported.
- Connecting every poll produced a jumpy line. The redraw showed the trend. On that non-zero line she cites Francis Gagnon: leave at least a third of the plot free under the line.
- A chart that put two series on baselines at opposite edges of the frame was split into two charts. The relationship survived, and each series kept a baseline.
- Recolouring some points inside one group read as a second category. Colour changes category. Emphasis inside a group needs another device.

Other public Economist notes used, not on the shortlist:

- Elizabeth Lees, [Tips for visualising data like The Economist](https://education.economist.com/blog/interviews/tips-for-visualising-data-like-the-economist) (Economist Education, 27 February 2024). Dark blue and light blue as the working pair, a ceiling around six colours, party colours as the exception, highlight by accent, tint, or bold type. Bubble charts need labels because area is a poor encoding. Thermometer charts compare two categories in a small space.
- Helen Atkinson, [Charting new territory](https://medium.economist.com/charting-new-territory-7f5afb293270) (31 January 2020). Instagram charts: red `#EC111A` as the highlight, quieter blues behind, party and some map colours overriding the template. When the print chart will not fit, pull the takeaway rather than shrinking everything.
- Alex Selby-Boothroyd, [A year in Graphic detail](https://medium.economist.com/a-year-in-graphic-detail-d1825b28e06f) (1 November 2019). The section launched in October 2018. "Hold the page", with a target of about twice as much visualisation as text. ggplot in R was the main sketching tool. Unconventional forms were welcome when they were clear. The same note lists 3D pie charts among forms not yet used. This skill does not adopt 3D pies.
- Rosamund Pearce, [Why you sometimes need to break the rules in data viz](https://medium.economist.com/why-you-sometimes-need-to-break-the-rules-in-data-viz-4d8ece284919). A truncated axis can exaggerate. Some small, meaningful differences disappear if the axis starts at zero. They signpost a broken scale with a symbol, except on index charts where 100 is the baseline. Do not break a bar or column axis. The 2019 style used a thermometer instead of a column when the numerical axis had to break.

From the GIJN account of Burn-Murdoch:

- A bare chart lost, in the study he cited, to a chart with a narrative title and several well-placed notes. Annotation is for the general reader.
- Readers in the eye-tracking study he cited hit the title, then the axes, then the data. Text is what they remember.
- Direct labels replaced a legend that forced a shuttle between the key and the lines.
- One contrasting series in a field of grey. A colour on every line means nothing pops.
- The COVID chart he walked through kept its geometry across revisions. The gains came from the title, the labels, the grouping of secondary countries in grey, and the notes.
- FT charts are often recognised by a salmon-pink ground. That is his column's look. It is not the default for an Economist-style piece. Use it only when the brief is an FT graphic.

[FT Visual Vocabulary](https://ft-interactive.github.io/visual-vocabulary/): choose the relationship first. The categories are deviation, correlation, change over time, ranking, distribution, part-to-whole, magnitude, spatial, and flow. The site says the list is neither exhaustive nor a wizard. It is inspired by the Graphic Continuum of Jon Schwabish and Severino Ribecca. The poster itself is not copied into this skill. Repo: [Financial-Times/chart-doctor](https://github.com/Financial-Times/chart-doctor/tree/main/visual-vocabulary).

*How Charts Work* is the book-length statement of those principles. Nothing in this skill claims a reading of the book.

## New York Times Graphics

| | |
| --- | --- |
| Amanda Cox | Graphics editor at The New York Times, later editor of The Upshot from 2016 until she left in 2022, according to her [Times byline page](https://www.nytimes.com/by/amanda-cox). Handle @amandacox. |
| Archie Tse | Promoted to graphics director on 28 October 2019 ([Times Company note](https://www.nytco.com/press/archie-tse-promoted-to-graphics-director/)). The interaction rules below are from his Malofiej 2016 talk, when he was deputy graphics director. Handle @archietse. |

Cox, [Simply Statistics interview, 1 June 2012](https://simplystatistics.org/posts/2012-06-01-interview-with-amanda-cox-graphics-editor-at-the-new/):

- A graphic that "feels like homework" has failed. Do not assume the reader already cares. Drop jargon. The graphic should carry its point without the article.
- Typography is hierarchy and clarity.
- Annotate a few points on a scatter so the reader can learn the form from examples.
- The words should state the pattern or an expert's reading, not announce that data exists.
- She sketches forms in R, then edits print output. The sketch is not the finished graphic.
- She liked sliders that imply a beginning, middle, and end. See the conflict with Tse below.

Cox, [Ohio State lecture abstract](https://stat.osu.edu/events/wherry-lecture-amanda-cox): distributions are more interesting than averages, context series should support rather than lead, and the annotation layer matters. The abstract is the source. A secondhand write-up of an Eyeo talk was not used.

Tse, [Malofiej 2016 slides](https://github.com/archietse/malofiej-2016) (PDF in that repo):

- Readers scroll. Steppers, tabs, and sliders were often a way to hide more material, and readers did not reach it.
- If the reader must do something other than scroll, something spectacular has to happen.
- Assume nobody sees a tooltip. Important content stays visible.
- Making an interactive work on every platform is expensive.
- The shift he described: fewer small embedded graphics, more standalone visual stories, more edited text, motion triggered by scroll when motion is needed, and a high bar for true interactives.

## Conflicts to resolve in the open

Pick the source that matches the piece, and name it. Drop the other rule rather than averaging them.

| Topic | Public sources | What to do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lead colour | Lees (2024): dark blue and light blue, hexes unpublished. Atkinson (2020, Instagram): red `#EC111A` as the highlight, blues behind. Election design sheet: brand red `#E3120B`. | One accent. Use `#E3120B` only as a brand nod, and do not paint every mark with it. Use `#EC111A` only when matching Atkinson's note. Otherwise pick one semantic accent and say so. Do not invent a "house blue" hex. |
| Broken axes | Leo: truncated bars mislead; a non-zero line should keep air underneath. Pearce: a signed break is sometimes required so a real difference is visible; never break a bar or column; index charts need no break symbol. | Bars from zero. Lines may break when zero would hide the story, with a mark and air under the line. A thermometer is the Economist alternative Pearce describes, not a licence to clip a normal column. |
| How much text | Tufte-style minimalism versus Burn-Murdoch: bare charts lost to titled, annotated ones in the study he cited. Economist print pages are dense and still quiet. | Cut decoration. Keep the title, the direct labels, and a small number of notes. |
| Interaction | Cox in 2012 on sliders. Tse in 2016: readers did not reach slider and tab content. | The default view carries the finding. A control may add a cut the title has named. It may not be the only place the finding exists. |
| 3D | Selby-Boothroyd lists 3D pies among unused forms. Standard chart practice treats 3D as distortion. | No 3D. The parenthetical is not a house rule. |
