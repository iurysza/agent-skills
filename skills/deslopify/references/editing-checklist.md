# Editing checklist

Treat these as diagnostic signals, not forbidden forms. One instance may work; repetition creates the synthetic effect.

## Meaning and evidence

- Replace vague authorities such as "experts" or "reports" with a named source, or remove the attribution.
- Cut claims of importance that the paragraph does not demonstrate.
- Preserve uncertainty. Do not turn "may" into "will" to make a sentence punchier.
- Prefer concrete actors and actions over abstractions that hide who did what.

## Diction

- Replace inflated verbs and nouns with ordinary precise ones.
- Remove adjectives and adverbs that add mood but no information.
- Watch clusters of fashionable words such as "robust", "seamless", "transformative", and "ecosystem".
- Use `is`, `has`, and `does` when ceremonial substitutes add nothing.

## Sentence rhythm

- Break repeated contrast formulas such as "not X but Y".
- Remove strings of fragments used only for drama.
- Vary openings when several sentences begin the same way.
- Keep lists only when the items genuinely belong together; do not force every point into three parts.
- Avoid rhetorical questions that answer themselves without helping the reader reason.

## Paragraph structure

- Give each paragraph one job, but let sentence lengths vary naturally.
- Remove paragraphs that merely announce, summarize, or celebrate the surrounding paragraphs.
- Collapse repeated examples that prove the same point.
- Replace disguised numbered lists with either a real list or connected prose.
- End when the argument is complete instead of restating it as a formal conclusion.

## Tone

- Remove manufactured suspense before ordinary facts.
- Do not tell the reader a claim is obvious, surprising, or important; show why.
- Avoid fake confession, canned vulnerability, and forced intimacy.
- Use analogies only when they clarify the mechanism.
- Keep stakes proportional to the evidence.

## Formatting

- Use bold for navigation or genuine emphasis, not at the start of every bullet.
- Reduce decorative punctuation and repeated em dashes.
- Prefer formatting the author already uses.
- Do not add headings, bullets, or emoji merely to make a short draft look structured.

## Final pass

Ask:

- Does this sound like one person addressing a specific audience?
- Can every claim be traced to evidence or clearly marked opinion?
- Did the edit preserve the author's intent and useful quirks?
- Are any sentence shapes repeated often enough to become noticeable?
- Can a sentence, example, or conclusion disappear without losing meaning?
